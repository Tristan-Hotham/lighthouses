Wikidata knows of {{n.items|int}} lighthouses. I put every one of them on a map and switched them on. The coasts of the world draw themselves in light, the Victorians turn out to have built most of them, and the brightest coast of all belongs to Sweden, partly because Sweden wrote its lights down.

I have always had a soft spot for lighthouses. They are buildings with one job, done for strangers: a tower on the worst rock on the coast, so that nobody else has to be wrecked there. So I wanted to see all of them at once.

Wikidata, the free database that sits behind Wikipedia, has {{n.items|int}} lighthouses with a position, once I had taken out a few dozen keepers' cottages and replicas that had crept in. {{n.dated|int}} of them have a year when they were built or first lit. Give each one a soft glow on a dark map and this is what you get.

{{IMG|b_glow_world.png|World map on a dark navy background with every lighthouse in Wikidata drawn as a small amber glow. The coasts of Scandinavia, the Baltic, Britain, France, the Mediterranean and the north-eastern United States and Great Lakes glow brightly; Japan, China and eastern Australia are clearly lit; much of Africa, South Asia and South America shows only scattered points|Every lighthouse in Wikidata, one glow each. Where they crowd, the coast shines.}}

It looks like the Earth at night, and in a way it is: these are the lights a sailor would see. The North Atlantic is ringed with them. Japan and the Chinese coast are bright. Africa, much of South America and South and Southeast Asia are lit only here and there. Some of that pattern is real, the busy and dangerous seas of the industrial world, and some of it, as we will see, is about who has written their lights down.

The lighthouse century

Lighthouses are ancient, but most of the ones on the map are not. Only {{n.before1800|int}} of the dated lights were built before 1800. Then the industrial century arrived, with iron, steam, cheap glass, the Fresnel lens and a world trade that needed every headland lit. Outside Sweden, the number of new lights went from {{timeline.nosw_1820s|int}} in the 1820s to {{timeline.nosw_1870s|int}} in the 1870s, and it stayed above three hundred a decade until the First World War. The United States built most in the {{timeline.usa_peak_dec|raw}}s.

{{IMG|b_timeline.png|Stacked bar chart of lighthouses built per decade from 1700 to the 2020s, coloured by country. Bars rise from almost nothing before 1800 to about 300 to 500 a decade from the 1850s to the 1960s. Up to 1910 most of each bar is the rest of the world and the United States; from the 1930s to the 1960s most of each bar is blue for Sweden, peaking at 326 in the 1940s. After the 1970s the bars fall to about 100 to 220 a decade, and 51 in the 2020s so far|Lighthouses built per decade, by the date Wikidata gives for the building or first light. Blue is Sweden.}}

After the First World War the curve outside Sweden falls away: about {{timeline.nosw_1850_1909_avg|int}} new lights a decade from 1850 to 1909, about {{timeline.nosw_1970_2019_avg|int}} a decade from 1970 to 2019. The best sites were taken, and the work of a lighthouse was changing. Lights were automated through the twentieth century; in Britain the last keepers came ashore in 1998. Satellite navigation, fully working from the mid 1990s, now does much of what a light used to do. New lights tend to be small automatic beacons, and Wikidata rarely bothers with those.

Except in Sweden. Sweden has {{country.top.0.n|int}} lighthouses in Wikidata, {{country.swe_pct|pc}} of the world's total, and nearly all are dated. They peak in the {{timeline.swe_peak_dec|raw}}s, and from 1930 to 1969 Sweden alone supplies {{timeline.swe_share_1930_1969|pc}} of all the dated new lights in the database. Their recorded heights are small and {{timeline.swe_3069_sl0_pct|pc}} have no Wikipedia article. These look like the little automatic lights of the Swedish archipelagos, there because somebody loaded a whole national list. Other countries surely built similar lights; they just are not in the data.

{{IMG|b_glow_north.png|Night map of northern Europe with lighthouses as amber glows. The coasts of Sweden and Finland around the Baltic and the Gulf of Bothnia form a continuous bright line, much brighter than Norway, Denmark, Britain or the Low Countries|Northern Europe at night. The Swedish and Finnish coasts outshine everything else.}}

Counting coast

A country with more coast needs more lights, so raw counts are only half the picture. I measured the coast of every country on Natural Earth's 1:10m map, taking each country's outline and removing the borders it shares with neighbours, and divided its sea lights by that length. The world has about {{country.world_coast_km|int}} km of coast at that scale, and {{country.world_per1000|f1}} lighthouses in Wikidata per 1,000 km of it.

{{IMG|b_countries.png|Two horizontal bar charts. Left: lighthouses by country, led by Sweden with 2,555, then the United States 923, Finland 794, Canada 473 and the United Kingdom 468, with the part on lakes and rivers in blue. Right: sea lights per 1,000 km of coast, led by Sweden 341, Finland 126, Tunisia 85 and Germany 71, then the Netherlands, Portugal, France, Spain and the United Kingdom between about 39 and 52|Lighthouses by country, and per 1,000 km of coast.}}

Sweden comes out top again, with {{country.dense.0.per1000|int}} lights per 1,000 km, then Finland ({{country.dense.1.per1000|int}}), Tunisia and Germany. France, Spain and the United Kingdom sit together between about 39 and 48. At the bottom are the giants with vast, broken coasts: Canada has {{country.by_iso.CAN.per1000|f1}} per 1,000 km and Indonesia {{country.by_iso.IDN.per1000|f1}}. Indonesia has more than {{country.by_iso.IDN.n|int}} lighthouses, of course. Wikidata does not.

Measuring coast is a famous trap: the closer you look, the longer it gets. At this scale the Swedish skerries and the Norwegian fjords are smoothed away, so the numbers compare countries fairly but are not the "true" length of anything.

Doing the same for states, provinces and counties finds the most crowded coasts. All of the top ten are Swedish or Finnish, led by Västra Götaland on the Skagerrak with {{regions.top.0.per100|f1}} lights per 100 km. Outside those two countries, the densest are {{regions.top_outside_nordic.0.name|raw}}, on the shifting sandbanks of the Wadden Sea ({{regions.top_outside_nordic.0.per100|f1}}), and {{regions.top_outside_nordic.1.name|raw}}, where Long Island Sound is lit like a street ({{regions.top_outside_nordic.1.per100|f1}}).

Twelve Swedish and Finnish regions lead, from Västra Götaland at 71.9 down to about 21, then, in red, Lower Saxony 13.8, Connecticut 12.9, Bizerte 11.7, Finistère 11.0 and Livorno 10.9|The densest coasts: sea lights per 100 km, for regions with at least 150 km of coast.}}

The oldest, and the tallest

The oldest date in Wikidata belongs to a lighthouse that is long gone: the Pharos of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders, dated {{spot.Lighthouse of Alexandria.year|yr}}. The oldest still standing is the Tower of Hercules at A Coruña in Galicia, a Roman tower that Wikidata dates to {{spot.Tower of Hercules.year|yr}} and which still shines over the Atlantic. The Romans also left the Dubris Pharos inside Dover Castle ({{spot.Dubris Pharos.year|yr}}), a ruin now. Then come the medieval towers: Hook Head in Ireland ({{spot.Hook Lighthouse.year|yr}} in Wikidata, a date that looks rounded to a century), Kõpu in Estonia ({{spot.Kõpu Lighthouse.year|yr}}), Genoa's Lanterna ({{spot.Lighthouse of Genoa.year|yr}}) and Cordouan in the Gironde ({{spot.Cordouan Lighthouse.year|yr}}).

Old dates need care. Wikidata usually gives the year a light was first shown on the site, and towers were rebuilt: the Planier light off Marseille carries a date of 1326, but its present tower went up in 1959. Whether a tower still stands is not in the data I used, so I checked the best known of the oldest by hand, and the interactive page says where I could not be sure.

For height I used Wikipedia's list of tallest lighthouses, because the heights in my Wikidata extract had lost their units: Cape Hatteras appears as 210, which is its height in feet. The tallest traditional lighthouse is the granite tower on the Île Vierge in Finistère, {{tallest.top.m|int}} m, finished in {{tallest.top.year|raw}}. {{tallest.stone_or_brick|int}} of the {{tallest.n|int}} towers of 60 m or more are stone, granite or brick.

What the map cannot see

Wikidata's coverage varies enormously from country to country. Some national lists have been loaded almost light by light, often without an English name or a Wikipedia article: {{country.by_iso.SWE.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of Sweden's lights have at most one article, as do {{country.by_iso.FIN.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of Finland's. Other countries are covered by volunteers writing one article at a time; in the United States only {{country.by_iso.USA.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of lights lack a second article. And some coastal countries have no lighthouse in Wikidata at all, Greenland, Nigeria, Kenya and Ecuador among them.

{{IMG|b_coverage.png|Horizontal bar chart of the share of each country's lighthouses with at most one Wikipedia article. Madagascar, Sweden, Peru, Mexico, Finland, China, Egypt and Tunisia are all above 80 percent, in red; Argentina, the United States, Portugal, Croatia, Spain and Estonia are below 20 percent, in blue|How bare are the records? A high share hints at a bulk import from a national list.}}

So the bright coasts on the map are bright for two reasons at once: busy, dangerous water, and a record that someone has taken the trouble to put into open data. The dark coasts are not empty of lights. They are empty of records, and that is something anyone can fix.

How I did this

I took every Wikidata item that is a lighthouse, or a kind of lighthouse, and has coordinates ({{n.items_raw|int}} items), and removed {{n.dropped|int}} keepers' houses, barrack blocks and replicas and one item with no known position. Each light went to the Natural Earth country it stands in, or the nearest within {{meta.near_km|int}} km for lights on offshore rocks. Lights more than {{meta.sea_km|int}} km inland from the sea coast ({{n.inland|int}}, mostly on the Great Lakes and the Swedish and Finnish lakes) are left out of the per-coast figures. Dates are Wikidata's earliest inception year. The interactive page lets you search every light, colour them by date, and switch between a day map and the night glow. The paper sets out the method and its limits in full.
