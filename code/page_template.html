Lighthouses of the world

Wikidata knows of {{n.items|int}} lighthouses. Put every one on a map and light it, and the world's coasts draw themselves: thick and bright around Scandinavia, Britain and New England, thin and dark along much of Africa and Asia. Some of that is the sea, and some of it is who wrote the lights down.

Lighthouses are the most hopeful buildings we have: a tower put up on the worst rock on the coast so that nobody else has to die there. The Romans built them, the Victorians built them by the thousand, and now, with satellite navigation in every wheelhouse, we mostly keep them for love. Wikidata, the free database behind Wikipedia, records {{n.items|int}} of them with a position, and {{n.dated|int}} with a year they were built or first lit.

This page puts them all on one map, counts them by country and by the length of each country's coast, follows the building boom decade by decade, and lists the oldest and the tallest. It is also a study of a record. Sweden alone has {{country.top.0.n|int}} of the lighthouses, {{country.swe_pct|pc}} of the world's total, because a national list of Swedish lights has made its way into Wikidata almost light by light. Other countries with long, dangerous coasts barely appear. I will try to keep the two stories apart.

    02 · WhenThe lighthouse century, and after

    Lighthouses are ancient, but most of the ones on the map are Victorian or younger. Of the {{n.dated|int}} dated lights, only {{n.before1800|int}} were built before 1800. Then the industrial century arrives: iron, steam, cheap glass and the Fresnel lens, and maritime trade that needed every headland lit. Outside Sweden, new lights went from {{timeline.nosw_1820s|int}} in the 1820s to {{timeline.nosw_1870s|int}} in the {{timeline.peak_dec_nosw|raw}}s, and stayed above three hundred a decade until the First World War. The United States built most in the {{timeline.usa_peak_dec|raw}}s ({{timeline.usa_peak_n|int}}).

    After 1910 the curve outside Sweden falls away: about {{timeline.nosw_1850_1909_avg|int}} new lights a decade from 1850 to 1909, about {{timeline.nosw_1970_2019_avg|int}} a decade from 1970 to 2019, a fall of {{timeline.nosw_drop_x|f1}} times. The good sites were taken, and the job of the lighthouse was changing. Lights were automated through the twentieth century, keepers left (in Britain the last of them came off duty in 1998), and satellite navigation, fully working from the mid 1990s, took over much of the work of finding a ship's position. New lights now tend to be small, unmanned beacons, and Wikidata rarely bothers with those.

    Sweden tells a different story, or rather its record does. Sweden's {{country.top.0.n|int}} lights are nearly all dated, and they peak in the {{timeline.swe_peak_dec|raw}}s, with {{timeline.swe_peak_n|int}}. From 1930 to 1969 Sweden supplies {{timeline.swe_share_1930_1969|pc}} of all dated new lights in Wikidata. Their recorded heights are small (a median of {{timeline.swe_3069_h_median|f1}}, against {{timeline.nosw_5009_h_median|f1}} for lights built elsewhere from 1850 to 1909, most of them in metres) and {{timeline.swe_3069_sl0_pct|pc}} have no Wikipedia article at all. These look like the small automatic lights that lit the Swedish archipelagos in the twentieth century, recorded because someone loaded a full national list. Elsewhere the same kind of light is simply missing.

    03 · WhereCounting lights, and counting coast

    By raw count, the top five are Sweden ({{country.top.0.n|int}}), the United States ({{country.by_iso.USA.n|int}}), Finland ({{country.by_iso.FIN.n|int}}), Canada ({{country.by_iso.CAN.n|int}}) and the United Kingdom ({{country.by_iso.GBR.n|int}}). But a country with more coast needs more lights, so I also divide each country's sea lights by the length of its coast, measured on Natural Earth's 1:10m map: the outline of the country, minus every border it shares with a neighbour. The world has about {{country.world_coast_km|int}} km of coast at that scale and {{country.world_per1000|f1}} lights in Wikidata per 1,000 km of it.

At the bottom are countries with vast, island-strewn coasts: Canada has {{country.by_iso.CAN.per1000|f1}} per 1,000 km, Russia {{country.by_iso.RUS.per1000|f1}} and Indonesia {{country.by_iso.IDN.per1000|f1}}.     04 · OldestTwo thousand years of keeping a light

    The oldest date in Wikidata belongs to a lighthouse that is long gone: the Pharos of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders, dated {{spot.Lighthouse of Alexandria.year|yr}} and brought down by earthquakes in the Middle Ages. The oldest that still stands, and still shines, is the Tower of Hercules at A Coruña in Galicia, a Roman tower dated {{spot.Tower of Hercules.year|yr}}. The Romans also left the Dubris Pharos inside Dover Castle (dated {{spot.Dubris Pharos.year|yr}}), still standing though long dark. Then come the medieval towers: Hook Head in Ireland ({{spot.Hook Lighthouse.year|yr}} in Wikidata, a date that looks rounded to a century), Kõpu in Estonia ({{spot.Kõpu Lighthouse.year|yr}}), the Lanterna of Genoa (rebuilt {{spot.Lighthouse of Genoa.year|yr}}) and Cordouan in the Gironde ({{spot.Cordouan Lighthouse.year|yr}}).

    The oldest dated lighthouses in Wikidata

    Years are Wikidata's inception dates. Whether a tower still stands is not in Wikidata's record: I marked the best known by hand, and say "not checked" where I could not be sure. Click a row to see it on the map.

    Treat the old dates with care. Wikidata usually gives the year a light was first shown on the site, and old towers were often rebuilt on the same spot: the Genoese Lighthouse at Constanța is dated to the Genoese era but the tower is from 1860, and the Planier light off Marseille is dated 1326 though the present tower went up in 1959. Many early dates are rounded to a century.

    05 · TallestThe tallest towers

    For height I use Wikipedia's list of tallest lighthouses, which gives {{tallest.n|int}} traditional towers of 60 m or more, measured from the ground. The tallest is the Île Vierge lighthouse off Finistère, {{tallest.top.m|int}} m of granite, finished in {{tallest.top.year|raw}}. {{tallest.stone_or_brick|int}} of the {{tallest.n|int}} are stone, granite or brick, and {{tallest.n_before1900|int}} are from the nineteenth century or earlier.

    Why not use Wikidata's heights?

    Wikidata has a height for {{n.with_height|int}} lighthouses, but the extract I worked from kept the numbers and lost their units. Of the {{tallest.matched|int}} towers in the Wikipedia list that I could match, {{tallest.wd_unit.metres|int}} have a Wikidata height in metres and {{tallest.wd_unit.feet|int}} (Cape Hatteras, 210) in feet. So on the map each record shows the Wikidata number with a warning, and the ranking uses the Wikipedia list only.

    06 · What is missingA map of the record as much as of the sea

    Wikidata's coverage of lighthouses is very uneven. Some countries' lights arrived in bulk from a national list, many of them without an English name or a Wikipedia article: {{country.by_iso.SWE.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of Sweden's lights have at most one Wikipedia article, as do {{country.by_iso.FIN.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of Finland's and {{country.by_iso.TUN.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of Tunisia's, and {{coverage.swe_no_label|int}} Swedish lights have no English label at all. Other countries are covered light by light, article by article, by volunteers: in the United States only {{country.by_iso.USA.sl_le1_pct|pc}} of lights lack a second article. And some coastal countries have no lighthouse in Wikidata at all, among them Greenland, Nigeria, Kenya, Ecuador and the United Arab Emirates.

    So read the map twice. Its bright coasts are where the sea is busy and dangerous, and where lighthouse lists have found their way into open data. Europe holds {{coverage.by_continent.0.pct|pc}} of the lights; Africa {{coverage.by_continent.3.pct|pc}}, South America {{coverage.by_continent.4.pct|pc}}. The dark coasts are not empty of lights. They are empty of records.

    A lighthouse map is a map of danger, of trade and of whoever wrote the lights down.

    07 · How I did thisMethods and caveats

    Lighthouses. Every Wikidata item that is an instance of lighthouse or one of its subclasses and has coordinates, through the QLever service: {{n.rows|int}} rows, one item per row and value, giving {{n.items_raw|int}} items. I removed {{n.dropped|int}} keepers' houses, barrack blocks and replicas and {{n.nocoord|int}} item whose position is recorded as unknown, leaving {{n.items|int}}. Where an item has several inception dates ({{n.multi_year|int}}) I use the earliest. Labels are English; {{n.no_label|int}} lights ({{n.no_label_pct|pc}}) have none and are shown by their Wikidata number.

    Countries and coasts. Each light is placed in the Natural Earth 1:10m country it stands in, or the nearest within {{meta.near_km|int}} km for lights on rocks offshore; {{n.how.wikidata|int}} lights further out take Wikidata's country. Natural Earth's map units are used as they are, so Greenland, Puerto Rico and the Faroes count on their own (I merged Åland into Finland). A light more than {{meta.sea_km|int}} km inland from the sea coast is a lake or river light ({{n.inland|int}}, mostly on the Great Lakes and the Swedish and Finnish lakes) and is left out of the per-coast figures. Coast length is the part of each 1:10m country or region outline not shared with a neighbour, measured on the ellipsoid. Coastlines are fractal: at this scale fjords and skerries are smoothed, so the lengths compare countries fairly but are not the "true" length of any coast.

    Oldest. Dates are Wikidata's inception years, unchanged. Wikidata does not say in this extract whether a tower still stands; I marked the {{n.oldest_checked|int}} best known of the oldest lights by hand from their published histories and the Wikipedia list. Tallest. Heights and years from Wikipedia's List of tallest lighthouses (revision {{tallest.revid|raw}}), which follows the Lighthouse Directory's definition of a traditional lighthouse.
